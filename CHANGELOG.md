# Changelog

Items marked with ⚠️ are breaking changes.

## Unreleased

### Added

- Added support for architectures other than x86-64 and AArch64, such as wasm32 and riscv64, with a portable backend that does not change the rounding mode.
- Added 32-bit x86 Linux (`i686-unknown-linux-gnu`) and x86-64 Windows with the MSVC toolchain to the targets that CI tests. CI runs the unit and integration tests there, but not those that compare with MPFR, which are compiled only on 64-bit Linux and macOS.
- Added AArch64 Linux (`aarch64-unknown-linux-gnu`) to the targets that CI tests, under QEMU's user-mode emulation. CI runs the unit tests there, among them those that compare with MPFR, those that compare the AArch64 backend with the portable one, and those that compare the portable one with operations that round with the rounding mode switched in FPCR.
- Added the Cargo feature `force-generic`, which uses the portable backend on x86-64 and AArch64 too, and on x86-64 keeps the multi-precision kernel from detecting the CPU at run time.
- Added a backend for WebAssembly that uses 128-bit SIMD instructions, enabled by the target feature `simd128`. It makes most interval operations other than multiplication 1.6 to 1.9 times as fast as the portable backend, and multiplication 1.3 to 1.5 times as fast.
- Added a backend for x86-64 processors that have SSE4.2 but neither AVX nor FMA, which lowers the lowest supported processor from Haswell to Nehalem. Like the WebAssembly backend, it does not change the rounding mode. On such a processor, it makes most interval operations 1.6 to 2.5 times as fast as the portable backend.
- Added correctly rounded `f64` functions in the module `inari::cr`: `exp`, `exp2`, `exp10`, `ln`, `log2`, `log10`, `sin`, `cos`, `tan`, `asin`, `acos`, `atan`, `atan2`, `sinh`, `cosh`, `tanh`, `asinh`, `acosh`, `atanh`, `pow` and `powi`, each rounded to nearest with ties to even, toward -∞ and toward +∞, such as `cr::exp_rn`, `cr::exp_rd` and `cr::exp_ru`. They give MPFR's values at the zeros, at the infinities and outside the domains, and `pow` gives those of C99 and MPFR at a finite x < 0 and at x = -∞. Each settles some arguments by rules, and the others by Ziv's strategy with enclosures of up to 4,096 bits. At an argument that reaches that cap with no rounding test passing, a function returns the rounding of a bound of the last enclosure, which is not known to be correctly rounded, but none of the 9,949,560 hard-to-round arguments that CORE-MATH lists for these functions reaches it. Tests on 64-bit Linux and macOS compare them with MPFR in all three roundings, at a sample of those arguments among others.
- Added correctly rounded `cbrt`, `hypot` and `sqrt` to `inari::cr`, each rounded to nearest with ties to even, toward -∞ and toward +∞, such as `cr::cbrt_rn`, `cr::hypot_rd` and `cr::sqrt_ru`. `cbrt` and `hypot` first try a fast path in `f64`, proved in Rocq to return the result rounded wherever it returns one, and only where it does not decide the rounding compute an exact integer cube or square root with the kernel, without Ziv's strategy and so with no cap. They give MPFR's values at the zeros and the infinities, including `hypot(±∞, y) = +∞` for a NaN `y`. `sqrt_rn` is `f64::sqrt`, and `sqrt_rd` and `sqrt_ru` are those of the backend of the interval arithmetic. Tests on 64-bit Linux and macOS compare them with MPFR in all three roundings, and `cbrt` and `hypot` at a sample of the hard-to-round arguments that CORE-MATH lists for them. On x uniform in [-10, 10), and on x of a random sign with a uniformly random exponent from -1074 to 1023 and significand, a call of `cbrt` of `cr` takes 1.4 to 1.5 times as many instructions as the correctly rounded `cbrt` of CORE-MATH in the same rounding, as counted with cachegrind. A call of `hypot` takes 1.4 to 1.5 times as many as that of CORE-MATH on x and y uniform in [-10, 10), and 1.9 to 2.2 times as many on x and y of random signs with uniformly random exponents from -1074 to 1023 and significands, drawn again until their binary exponents differ by 27 or more, where it computes a square root only for a few pairs with a subnormal argument. Arguments that the fast path declines, such as exact results rounded toward -∞ or +∞ (`cr::cbrt_rd(8.0)`, `cr::hypot_ru(3.0, 4.0)`), results of `hypot` midway between two `f64`s rounded to nearest, and `hypot` of two subnormals, still take the kernel's path: on exact cubes and on scaled Pythagorean pairs, rounded toward -∞ or +∞, a call of `cbrt` takes 5.6 to 5.7 times as many instructions as that of CORE-MATH, and one of `hypot` 15 times as many.
- Added `Interval::cbrt`, `Interval::hypot`, `DecInterval::cbrt` and `DecInterval::hypot`, IEEE 1788's recommended functions rootn(x, 3) and hypot(x, y), which compute their bounds with the functions of the kernel that `cr::cbrt_rd`, `cr::cbrt_ru`, `cr::hypot_rd` and `cr::hypot_ru` call, and return the tightest interval at every argument. `hypot` of a bounded box can overflow, as in `hypot([f64::MAX, f64::MAX], [f64::MAX, f64::MAX])` = [`f64::MAX`, +∞], which `DecInterval::hypot` decorates `dac`, as IEEE 1788 asks.

Every token gives the same intervals and decorations; only the speed differs.

### Changed

- ⚠️ Bumped the minimum supported Rust version to 1.86.0.
- Made the default backend for x86-64 processors with AVX and FMA compute the directed roundings explicitly, instead of switching the rounding mode around every instruction. No backend for x86-64 changes the rounding mode any more. On a Zen 2 processor, interval addition and subtraction take 0.47 times as long as before, multiplication 0.49 to 0.62 (0.78 to 0.79 where the products are below 2^-968), division 0.67 to 0.69, evaluating a degree-8 polynomial 0.41 to 0.45, and a 3×3 determinant 0.33. Only the latency of a chain of dependent `add_ru`, `div_ru` and `mul_add_ru` operations became slightly worse, by 3 to 12 percent.
- Made the AArch64 backend compute the directed roundings with error-free transformations and the fused multiply-add, as the default backend for x86-64 does, instead of switching the rounding mode in FPCR around every operation, with the same results as before. No backend changes the rounding mode any more. On an Apple M1 Pro, nearly all of the time of these operations had gone to the writes to FPCR, as a prototype of this backend showed.

- Made interval literals parse without GMP and MPFR, with a big integer of inari's own. That the magnitude of a number is above 2^1024 or below 2^-1074, the smallest positive `f64`, is now told from the numbers of bits of its numerator and denominator and from its exponents where these suffice, without computing its power of ten. On a Zen 2 processor, `[1e1900000000]` and `[1e-2147483648]`, whose powers of ten have over 6 × 10^9 bits, parse in less than a microsecond, where GMP and MPFR took over 20 s and 2 GB of memory. In a crate that links `inari::cr`, built for the default `x86-64`, on a CPU with FMA, a call of `cos` of `cr` rounded toward -∞ or +∞ now takes 4.0 to 4.6 fewer instructions, as counted with cachegrind, on arguments uniform in [0.1, 10), in [-10, 10) and in [-π/4, π/4), on arguments of either sign with a magnitude log-uniform in [2^-30, 2^1024) and in [2^60, 2^1024), and on the `f64`s nearest to the multiples of π/2 other than 0, 2.3 fewer on those with a magnitude log-uniform in [2^-1074, 2^1024), and 7 fewer on 4,096 arguments of random sign whose magnitudes are the `f64`s nearest to 2^-26. On the hard-to-round cases of CORE-MATH that the tests use, it takes 1.7 more or 11.5 fewer, at two starts of the stack 16 bytes apart, as a call of the C library's `memset` that clears a scratch buffer in the unchanged slow path takes 42 or 49 instructions by the address of the buffer, and 4.9 fewer outside `memset` at both. The same holds in a build for `x86-64-v2`. On a CPU without FMA, a call takes 2.0 to 2.6 fewer where one on a CPU with FMA takes 4.0 to 4.6 fewer, 1.3 fewer on the log-uniform arguments in [2^-1074, 2^1024), 7 fewer on those next to 2^-26, and 2.9 more or 10.4 fewer on the hard-to-round cases, 3.7 fewer outside `memset`. Built for Haswell, which clears that buffer without a call of `memset`, it takes 4.5 fewer on the arguments next to 2^-26, 1.9 fewer on the hard-to-round cases, and as many as before, or at most 0.1 fewer, on the other sets. A call of `cos` rounded to nearest takes as many as before in each of these builds.
- Made sin x and tan x of the multi-precision kernel, rounded toward -∞ or +∞, compute their rules for arguments of magnitude in [2^-26, 2^-13) for sin and in [2^-26, 2^-14) for tan, which round x - x^3/6 and x + x^3/3, from the cube of the argument's significand with shifts of 64-bit integers where they shifted 128-bit integers by amounts that depend on the argument, and with a comparison in place of a second division, with the same results as before. The copies of the two functions built for `x86-64` and `x86-64-v2` then save one register that the function must save where they saved three, and those built for Haswell none, where sin saved three and tan one. In a crate that links `inari::cr`, built for the default `x86-64`, on a CPU with FMA, a call of `sin` of `cr` rounded toward -∞ or +∞ now takes 5.0 to 5.1 fewer instructions, as counted with cachegrind, on the sets of arguments on which one of `cos` takes 4.0 to 4.6 fewer in the entry above, 2.5 fewer on the log-uniform arguments in [2^-1074, 2^1024) and 7.2 fewer on those next to 2^-26, and a call of `tan` 4.9 to 5.1, 2.6 and 7.7 fewer. The same holds in a build for `x86-64-v2` and on a CPU without FMA. In these builds, on the hard-to-round cases, a call of `sin` takes about 50 more or 60 fewer and one of `tan` 96.1 more or 105.5 fewer, at the two starts of the stack, by the cost of `memset` as for cos, and about 5 fewer outside `memset` at both. Built for Haswell, a call of `sin` takes 7.4 to 8.5 fewer on those sets, 4.4 fewer on the log-uniform arguments in [2^-1074, 2^1024), 8.7 fewer on those next to 2^-26 and 7.3 fewer on the hard-to-round cases, and one of `tan` 2.5 to 2.6, 1.4, 8.5 and 3.0 fewer. Calls of `sin` and `tan` rounded to nearest take as many as before in each of these builds.
- Deprecated the Cargo feature `gmp`, which now does nothing and is no longer enabled by default, and the features `gmp-mpfr-sys`, `nom` and `rug`, which Cargo derived from the optional dependencies that `gmp` enabled, and which now do nothing either. They will be removed in the next major release. `nom` is now a dependency of every build.

### Fixed

- Fixed `(Dec)Interval::rad` to return `0.0` instead of `-0.0` for the interval [0, 0] when its upper bound is `-0.0`, as in `const_interval!(0.0, -0.0)`. It returned `-0.0` with Rust 1.96 on x86-64, because `f64::max` may return either zero when given `0.0` and `-0.0`.
- Fixed `(Dec)Interval::cancel_minus` and `(Dec)Interval::cancel_plus` to return the tightest interval instead of `(Dec)Interval::ENTIRE` for some arguments whose widths round to the same float and that have a bound of `f64::MIN` or `f64::MAX`, such as `x.cancel_minus(x)` for `x` = [`f64::MIN`, -3 × 2^970], which is [0, 0]. An intermediate result of the error-free sum that compares the widths overflowed.
- Fixed `(Dec)Interval::cos`, `(Dec)Interval::sin` and `(Dec)Interval::tan` to return the tightest interval, and `DecInterval::tan` the strongest decoration, as the documentation states, also where a bound of the argument is close to a multiple of π/2. They located the multiples of π/2 in the argument by dividing it by `Interval::PI` or `Interval::FRAC_PI_2`, which only encloses the quotient, so that a bound close to such a multiple, relative to its magnitude, could give a wider result. For example, `tan` of [7.853981633974484, 7.853981633974485], just above the pole 5π/2, was [-∞, +∞], decorated `trv`, and is now [-1718163698371049, -680181816043107], decorated `com`. Where the division cannot tell, they now find the multiples from the signs of the sine and the cosine of the bounds, which the multi-precision kernel (see above) rounds correctly wherever one of its rules or a rounding test of Ziv's strategy settles them.
- Fixed `{:.0x}` of `(Dec)Interval`s to print the exact digit of a positive upper bound or a negative lower bound of at most 4 significant bits, which MPFR printed one too large, as in `[0x8p-3,0x9p-3]` for [1, 1], or, where that made 16, as `0x` alone, as in `[0xfp+0,0x]` for [15, 15]. They are now `[0x8p-3,0x8p-3]` and `[0xfp+0,0xfp+0]`.
